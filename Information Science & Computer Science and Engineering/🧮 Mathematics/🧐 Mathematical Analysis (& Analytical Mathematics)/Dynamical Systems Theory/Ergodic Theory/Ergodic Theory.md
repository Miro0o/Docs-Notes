## Intro
> 🔗 https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_theory

**Ergodic theory** is a branch of [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") that studies [statistical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical "Statistical") properties of deterministic [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_system "Dynamical system"); it is the study of [ergodicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodicity "Ergodicity"). In this context, "statistical properties" refers to properties which are expressed through the behavior of [time averages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_average "Time average") of various functions along trajectories of dynamical systems. The notion of deterministic dynamical systems assumes that the equations determining the dynamics do not contain any [random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random "Random") perturbations, [noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noisy_data "Noisy data"), etc. Thus, the statistics with which we are concerned are properties of the dynamics.

Ergodic theory, like [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory"), is based on general notions of [measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_\(mathematics\) "Measure (mathematics)") theory. Its initial development was motivated by problems of [statistical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_physics "Statistical physics").

A central concern of ergodic theory is the behavior of a dynamical system when it is allowed to run for a long time. The first result in this direction is the [Poincaré recurrence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_recurrence_theorem "Poincaré recurrence theorem"), which claims that [almost all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_all "Almost all") points in any subset of the [phase space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space "Phase space") eventually revisit the set. Systems for which the Poincaré recurrence theorem holds are [conservative systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_system "Conservative system"); thus all ergodic systems are conservative.

More precise information is provided by various **ergodic theorems** which assert that, under certain conditions, the time average of a function along the trajectories exists [almost everywhere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_everywhere "Almost everywhere") and is related to the space average. Two of the most important theorems are those of [Birkhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_David_Birkhoff "George David Birkhoff") (1931) and [von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann") which assert the existence of a time average along each trajectory. For the special class of **ergodic systems**, this time average is the same for almost all initial points: statistically speaking, the system that evolves for a long time "forgets" its initial state. Stronger properties, such as [mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixing_\(mathematics\) "Mixing (mathematics)") and [equidistribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equidistribution "Equidistribution"), have also been extensively studied.

The problem of metric classification of systems is another important part of the abstract ergodic theory. An outstanding role in ergodic theory and its applications to [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_process "Stochastic process") is played by the various notions of [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure-theoretic_entropy "Measure-theoretic entropy") for dynamical systems.

The concepts of [ergodicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodicity "Ergodicity") and the [ergodic hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_hypothesis "Ergodic hypothesis") are central to applications of ergodic theory. The underlying idea is that for certain systems the time average of their properties is equal to the average over the entire space. Applications of ergodic theory to other parts of mathematics usually involve establishing ergodicity properties for systems of special kind. In [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), methods of ergodic theory have been used to study the [geodesic flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_flow "Geodesic flow") on [Riemannian manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_manifolds "Riemannian manifolds"), starting with the results of [Eberhard Hopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eberhard_Hopf "Eberhard Hopf") for [Riemann surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_surface "Riemann surface") of negative curvature. [Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain "Markov chain") form a common context for applications in [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory"). Ergodic theory has fruitful connections with [harmonic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_analysis "Harmonic analysis"), [Lie theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_theory "Lie theory") ([representation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_theory "Representation theory"), [lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_\(discrete_subgroup\) "Lattice (discrete subgroup)") in [algebraic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_group "Algebraic group")), and [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory") (the theory of [diophantine approximations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_approximations "Diophantine approximations"), [L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-functions "L-functions")).
